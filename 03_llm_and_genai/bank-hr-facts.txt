Employees in Austria are entitled to a minimum of 5 weeks (25 working days) of paid annual leave per year under the Urlaubsgesetz (Holiday Act).
After 25 years of service with the same employer, Austrian employees are entitled to 6 weeks (30 working days) of annual leave per year.
Unused vacation days in Austrian banks typically carry over to the following calendar year, but must be consumed within two years to avoid forfeiture.
In most Austrian banks, vacation requests must be submitted at least two weeks in advance and are subject to operational requirements.
Austrian labor law prohibits employers from denying vacation entirely; if a request is denied, an alternative date must be offered within the same leave year.
Austrian employees are entitled to paid leave on all 13 Austrian public holidays, including Nationalfeiertag on October 26 and Staatsfeiertag on May 1.
Employees working on a public holiday in Austria are entitled to a replacement day off in addition to their regular pay.
Austria's Mutterschutzgesetz (Maternity Protection Act) prohibits employment of pregnant employees 8 weeks before and 8 weeks after birth.
The post-birth maternity protection period extends to 12 weeks in the case of premature births, multiple births, or caesarean sections.
During maternity leave, Austrian employees receive Wochengeld (maternity allowance) equivalent to their average net income over the last 13 weeks before leave.
Wochengeld is paid by the Austrian Health Insurance Fund (ÖGK) and not directly by the employer.
After maternity leave, parents in Austria are entitled to take Karenz (parental leave) until the child's second birthday.
Both parents can take Karenz simultaneously or alternately, but Karenz must be taken in blocks of at least two months.
During Karenz, the employment relationship is suspended but the employee retains the right to return to the same or equivalent position.
Kinderbetreuungsgeld (childcare allowance) is paid by the state during Karenz and is separate from any employer top-up arrangements.
Many Austrian banks voluntarily offer an employer top-up (Zuschuss) during parental leave to supplement the state childcare allowance.
A father or second parent is entitled to a Papamonat (paternity month), an unpaid month off in the first 91 days after birth, with a state allowance of approximately €700.
The Papamonat requires notification to the employer at least 3 months before the expected birth date.
Employees returning from Karenz in Austria are protected from dismissal for 4 weeks after their return.
Dismissal protection (Kündigungsschutz) for pregnant employees begins when the employer is notified of the pregnancy and lasts until 4 weeks after the end of maternity leave.
Austrian banks are legally required to offer a part-time arrangement (Elternteilzeit) to employees returning from parental leave in companies with more than 20 employees.
Elternteilzeit can be taken until the child's 7th birthday or until school enrollment, whichever comes first.
In Austria, the standard maximum working week is 40 hours, with a legal cap of 50 hours including overtime.
With employee consent, working hours can be extended to up to 60 hours per week in exceptional cases under the Arbeitszeitgesetz.
Austrian employees are entitled to a minimum rest period of 11 consecutive hours between working days.
Bank employees in Austria are typically covered by the Kollektivvertrag für Angestellte der Banken und Bankiers (banking sector collective agreement).
The banking sector collective agreement (Bankenkollektivvertrag) is renegotiated annually, typically resulting in wage adjustments effective November 1.
Austrian bank employees receive a mandatory 13th monthly salary payment (Urlaubsgeld) in June.
A mandatory 14th monthly salary payment (Weihnachtsgeld) is paid to Austrian bank employees in November.
These 13th and 14th salary payments are partially tax-privileged in Austria under the so-called "Sechstelregelung," with the first sixth of annual salary taxed at a lower rate of 6%.
Overtime in Austrian banks is typically compensated either with additional pay (a minimum 50% surcharge) or with compensatory time off.
Austrian banks must provide employees with a written Dienstzettel (employment information document) within one month of employment commencement.
Probationary periods (Probezeit) in Austrian banks are typically limited to one month, during which either party may terminate the relationship without notice.
After the probationary period, notice periods in Austria range from 6 weeks to 5 months depending on length of service.
Employees in Austrian banks with more than 2 years of service are entitled to a minimum of 6 weeks' notice of termination by the employer.
Austrian law mandates that notice of termination by an employer can only take effect at the end of a calendar quarter, unless otherwise agreed.
Severance pay (Abfertigung) in Austria was replaced in 2003 by the "Mitarbeitervorsorge" system (also called Abfertigung Neu) for employees hired after January 1, 2003.
Under Abfertigung Neu, employers contribute 1.53% of monthly gross salary into an employee-managed provident fund (BV-Kasse).
Employees hired before January 1, 2003 may still be on the old severance system (Abfertigung Alt), which entitles them to up to 12 monthly salaries upon qualifying termination.
Sick pay (Entgeltfortzahlung) in Austrian banks is provided for a minimum of 6 to 12 weeks at full pay, depending on length of service.
After exhausting sick pay entitlement, employees receive half pay (50% of salary) for an additional 4 to 8 weeks.
The Austrian Social Insurance system covers employees against work-related accidents and occupational diseases through AUVA (Allgemeine Unfallversicherungsanstalt).
Austrian employees are entitled to 3 days of paid leave upon marriage or registered partnership, as provided by most bank collective agreements.
Employees in Austrian banks typically receive 2 days of paid bereavement leave upon the death of an immediate family member.
1 day of paid leave is usually granted for the birth of a child to the non-birthing partner under most bank collective agreements, in addition to the Papamonat.
Austrian banks typically offer employees the right to educational leave (Bildungskarenz) of up to 12 months for approved further education.
During Bildungskarenz, employees receive Weiterbildungsgeld from the AMS (Public Employment Service), amounting to the level of unemployment benefit.
Bildungskarenz must be agreed upon with the employer and requires the employee to have been employed for at least 6 months prior.
Sabbatical leave arrangements in Austrian banks are often structured as Freistellungsvereinbarungen and require individual negotiation.
Austrian banks are obligated to offer mandatory occupational health and safety training (Arbeitnehmerschutz) to all employees.
The Arbeiterkammer (Chamber of Labour) provides free legal advice to all Austrian employees on matters of employment law.
All Austrian employees are automatically members of the Arbeiterkammer; membership fees are deducted directly from salary.
Employees in Austrian banks with more than 5 employees are entitled to elect a Betriebsrat (works council).
The Betriebsrat in Austrian banks has co-determination rights (Mitbestimmungsrechte) in matters such as working time, social facilities, and certain HR policy changes.
Austrian banks must consult the Betriebsrat before implementing changes to bonus schemes, shift schedules, or mass layoffs.
Variable compensation in Austrian banks, including bonuses, is subject to a regulatory cap under EU Capital Requirements Directive (CRD IV), limiting variable pay to 100% of fixed salary (or 200% with shareholder approval) for identified staff.
Identified staff under CRD IV includes senior management, risk takers, and those with significant influence on the bank's risk profile.
At least 50% of variable pay for identified staff must be paid in instruments rather than cash, such as shares or share-linked instruments.
A portion of variable pay for identified staff must be deferred for a minimum of 3 to 5 years under EU banking remuneration rules.
Clawback provisions allow Austrian banks to reclaim already paid bonuses if material misconduct or risk failures are subsequently discovered.
Malus provisions allow Austrian banks to reduce or cancel unvested deferred compensation in cases of misconduct or poor risk outcomes.
Austrian banks are required to publish aggregated remuneration data for senior management and identified staff in their annual Pillar 3 disclosure reports.
External consulting work performed by bank employees in Austria typically requires prior written approval from the employer.
Employees in Austrian banks must disclose any secondary employment (Nebenbeschäftigung) that could conflict with their primary role or create a conflict of interest.
Engaging in competitive external consulting without employer approval may constitute grounds for dismissal for cause (Entlassung) in Austria.
Austrian bank employees are typically bound by confidentiality clauses that survive the termination of the employment contract.
Non-compete clauses (Konkurrenzklauseln) in Austrian employment law are only enforceable if the employee earns more than the monthly qualifying threshold set annually by statute.
Non-compete clauses in Austria cannot exceed a duration of one year and must be reasonable in geographical and subject-matter scope.
An employer wishing to enforce a non-compete clause may be required to pay compensation to the employee during the restriction period under certain collective agreements.
Whistleblower protection in Austria was strengthened with the implementation of the EU Whistleblowing Directive (2019/1937) via the Hinweisgeberschutzgesetz, effective 2023.
Austrian banks with 50 or more employees must establish an internal whistleblowing channel for employees to report compliance and legal violations.
Reports made in good faith through internal whistleblowing channels are legally protected; retaliation against whistleblowers is prohibited and can result in significant penalties.
Remote work (Homeoffice) arrangements in Austria are governed by a specific legal framework introduced in 2021 under the Homeoffice-Paket.
Austrian employees working from home are entitled to a cost contribution from their employer of up to €3 per day (maximum €300 per year) for home office expenses, which is tax-exempt.
Home office agreements must be documented in writing and both parties must consent; neither employer nor employee can be forced into a home office arrangement.
Equipment provided by the employer for home office use in Austria is exempt from benefit-in-kind taxation.
Employees working from home in Austria are protected by the same accident insurance (AUVA) as in the office, covering accidents that occur during home office hours.
Austrian banks typically have clearly defined remote work policies specifying eligible roles, maximum remote workdays per week, and security requirements.
Data protection in Austrian banks is governed by the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the Austrian Data Protection Act (Datenschutzgesetz, DSG).
Employee monitoring in Austrian workplaces is strictly limited; covert surveillance is prohibited and any monitoring system requires the agreement of the Betriebsrat.
Austrian banks are required to conduct regular data protection training for all employees with access to personal or customer data.
Employees in Austria have the right to request access to all personal data held about them by their employer under GDPR Article 15.
Austrian banks must have a designated Data Protection Officer (Datenschutzbeauftragter) in accordance with GDPR Article 37.
The statutory minimum wage in Austria is set through collective bargaining agreements; there is no universal statutory minimum wage in Austrian law.
The banking sector collective agreement ensures a sector-minimum starting salary, which is reviewed and adjusted annually.
Austrian bank employees may be entitled to a Jubiläumsgeld (long-service award) upon reaching specified milestones such as 10, 25, or 40 years with the employer.
Long-service awards in Austrian banks are partially exempt from tax and social security contributions up to defined thresholds.
Austrian banks typically offer subsidized staff meals, public transport subsidies (Jobticket), or other non-cash benefits that are partly tax-advantaged.
Employer contributions to company pension schemes (betriebliche Altersvorsorge) are deductible as a business expense and tax-exempt for the employee up to 10% of annual salary.
Company pension schemes in Austria are predominantly defined contribution plans managed by Pensionskassen or insurance companies.
Employees in Austria can make voluntary additional contributions to their BV-Kasse or pension plan via salary conversion (Gehaltsumwandlung).
The Austrian statutory retirement age is gradually increasing to 65 for all genders, aligned under a pension reform timeline running until 2033.
Early retirement options (Korridorpension) are available in Austria from age 62, subject to minimum insurance periods of 40 years.
Long-term care leave (Pflegekarenz) of up to 3 months is available to Austrian employees who need to care for a dependent family member.
During Pflegekarenz, employees receive a Pflegekarenzgeld allowance from the state.
Austrian bank employees have the right to reduce working hours (Pflegeteilzeit) for the purpose of caring for a dependent relative.
Part-time arrangements for care purposes (Familienhospizkarenz) allow Austrian employees to reduce or cease work temporarily to accompany a dying relative or a seriously ill child.
During Familienhospizkarenz, employees are protected from dismissal and receive limited state support.
Austrian law requires employers to make reasonable accommodations for employees with disabilities; companies with 25 or more employees must employ at least one person with a recognized disability or pay a compensatory levy (Ausgleichstaxe).
